One of the most common misconceptions about therapy — especially in Egypt, where the idea is still relatively new for many families — is that going to therapy means handing your decisions over to someone else. That a therapist will tell you what's right, what's wrong, or what to do next.

That's not what therapy is. And understanding why matters, because this single misunderstanding stops a lot of people from ever booking a first session.

## Why This Misconception Persists

It's an understandable assumption. In many other areas of life — medicine, law, religious guidance — we do go to an expert for a clear answer. So it's natural to assume therapy works the same way: you bring a problem, the therapist hands you a solution.

But every person has their own independent personality, values, feelings, and a unique way of being in the world. How could anyone else — however qualified — hand you a ready-made answer for how *you* specifically should live, decide, or feel?

## What Therapy Actually Does

Therapy isn't about being told what to do. It's about learning **how to think** — building the insight and understanding to reach your own answers, from your own internal compass, rather than someone else's.

A good therapist helps you:
- Identify new needs and priorities you may not have had language for before
- Recognize what's genuinely right for you, distinct from what you've been told is right
- Build the self-trust to act on your own judgment, rather than looking outward for validation

The therapist's role is to help you access your own insight — not to speak for you.

## Other Common Misconceptions Worth Addressing

**"Therapy is only for people in crisis."**
You don't need to hit rock bottom to benefit from therapy. It's just as valuable for building self-understanding, improving relationships, and developing self-confidence — long before a crisis ever arrives.

**"Going to therapy means something is wrong with me."**
Often, it's the opposite. Recognizing that you want to understand yourself better, break a repeating pattern, or handle a life transition with more support isn't a sign of weakness — it's a sign of self-awareness.

**"Talking to a friend is basically the same thing."**
Friendships are valuable, but they're rarely fully objective — they carry shared history, mutual bias, and social stakes that therapy doesn't. A therapist's role is also confidential and centered entirely on you, in a way friendships, by their nature, usually aren't.
