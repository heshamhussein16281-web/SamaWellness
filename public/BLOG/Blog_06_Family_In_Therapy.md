Many clients feel a real sense of guilt or discomfort when a conversation in therapy turns to their family — particularly when it involves describing something a parent did that caused pain. It can feel disloyal, even wrong, to speak about the people who raised you in anything but a positive light.

The honest answer is: it isn't wrong. And understanding why can make that part of the process feel far less fraught.

## The Halo Effect

We often see our parents through something psychologists call the **halo effect** — the same tendency that surrounds religious or deeply respected figures with an aura that places them above ordinary human standards. Parents can occupy a similar space in our minds: elevated, above scrutiny, protected from the kind of honest evaluation we'd apply to anyone else.

But strip away that halo, and what's left is simple: parents are people. People make mistakes. People carry unresolved experiences of their own — sometimes ones they were never able to fully address — and those experiences can, without any intention to harm, pass down to their children.

## Why This Reframing Matters

When you talk about your family in therapy, you're not passing judgment on them. **You're explaining your own history.**

Childhood experiences and our relationships with our parents shape our personalities, our emotional patterns, and how we approach relationships as adults, in ways that run deep. Understanding that history isn't an act of blame — it's a necessary part of understanding yourself. Once that history is understood, it becomes possible to recognize why we behave the way we do, and to start making more conscious choices going forward, rather than repeating inherited patterns without realizing it.

## What Therapy Actually Asks of You Here

Therapy is a judgment-free space. The goal was never to make you love your parents less, or to make you resent them more. The goal is to help you **understand** them — to understand their impact on you, and to accept that they, too, are human, with their own limitations.

This matters because the past shouldn't be the thing quietly controlling your present. Talking honestly about your family, in a private and supportive space, isn't betrayal. It's simply telling your story truthfully — and you have every right to tell it honestly, without shame or guilt.
