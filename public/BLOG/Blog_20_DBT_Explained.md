Some people feel things at full volume. A small criticism lands like an attack. An argument escalates in seconds. A wave of sadness or anger arrives so fast that, by the time it passes, you've already said or done something you regret.

If that sounds familiar, you're not "too sensitive," and you're not broken. You may simply never have been taught the skills to manage emotions that run intense. That's exactly what **DBT therapy**, or Dialectical Behavior Therapy, was built to teach.

## Where DBT Comes From

DBT was developed by psychologist Dr. Marsha Linehan, building on Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT). She found that people with very intense emotions often felt invalidated by therapy that focused only on changing their thoughts and behavior. They needed to feel understood first.

So DBT combines two ideas that might seem to pull in opposite directions. That's the "dialectical" part:

- **Acceptance:** your feelings make sense, given your history and your nervous system. You're doing the best you can.
- **Change:** and you can learn new ways of responding that serve you better.

Holding both at once, without having to choose between them, is the core of DBT.

## The Four DBT Skill Areas

DBT is very practical. It teaches concrete skills in four areas:

- **Mindfulness:** noticing what you're feeling and thinking in the moment, without immediately reacting to it. This is the foundation for everything else.
- **Distress tolerance:** getting through a crisis moment without making it worse. Techniques for riding out an intense urge or emotion until it passes.
- **Emotion regulation:** understanding your emotions, reducing how vulnerable you are to emotional swings, and changing emotions you don't want to stay stuck in.
- **Interpersonal effectiveness:** asking for what you need, saying no, and handling conflict while protecting both the relationship and your self-respect.

## Who DBT Can Help

DBT was originally developed for people with very intense, hard-to-manage emotions, and it's now used much more widely. It can be especially helpful if you:

- Experience emotions that feel overwhelming, sudden or hard to come down from
- Act impulsively when upset, and regret it later (often with anger)
- Have relationships that feel intense, unstable or full of conflict
- Struggle with anxiety or low mood alongside strong emotional reactions
- Turn to unhelpful ways of coping, such as overeating, overspending or shutting down, to escape difficult feelings

You don't need a specific diagnosis to benefit from DBT skills. Many people use them simply to feel calmer and more in control.

## What DBT Therapy Looks Like in a Session

In individual DBT-informed therapy, sessions usually combine:

- Looking at a recent difficult moment in detail, step by step, to understand what triggered it and where a different choice was possible
- Learning and practicing a specific skill, often with a small exercise to try between sessions
- Validation: making sense of why you reacted the way you did, before working on what to change

Progress is gradual and practical. Over time, most people notice they're reacting less automatically, recovering faster after hard moments, and feeling more like themselves.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What's the difference between DBT and CBT?** CBT focuses mainly on changing unhelpful thoughts and behaviors. DBT builds on CBT but adds acceptance, mindfulness and specific skills for very intense emotions and relationships.

**How long does DBT take?** Full DBT programs often run for six months to a year. DBT-informed individual therapy can be shorter, depending on your goals.

**Is DBT only for borderline personality disorder?** No. It was first developed for that, but DBT skills are now widely used for emotional regulation, impulsivity, relationship conflict, anxiety and low mood.
